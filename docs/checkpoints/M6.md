6. **M5 Wrapping Architecture**: The M5 single-agent planner remains intact; `MultiAgentCoordinator` seamlessly wraps it to arbitrate candidate moves and monitor dependencies.

## 3. Technical Implementation Details

### 3.1. ROS 2 Interface Messages (`amr_fleet_msgs`)
Four explicit ROS 2 message definitions were implemented, compiled into shared libraries, and verified:
1. `SpaceTimeReservation.msg`: Encapsulates reservation claims with robot ID, cell coordinates $(x, y)$, time step $t$, duration, reservation type (`VERTEX` or `EDGE`), and destination cell for edges.
2. `ConflictReport.msg`: Telemetry reporting conflict type (`VERTEX`, `EDGE_SWAP`, `WAITING`), participant robot IDs, conflict location, time step, and timestamp.
3. `DeadlockEvent.msg`: Telemetry reporting detected deadlock cycles, cycle duration, stall cycle count, selected victim robot ID, recovery action, and timestamp.
4. `CoordinationStatus.msg`: Real-time coordination status per AMR streaming state (`NOMINAL`, `WAITING`, `YIELDING`, `SIDESTEPPING`, `DEADLOCKED`), dynamic priority score, waiting-on robot ID, and active reservation count.

### 3.2. Space-Time Reservation System (`reservation_table.py`)
- Maintains dual hash tables for vertex reservations: $\mathcal{V} \times \mathcal{T} \to \text{Reservation}$ and edge transitions: $\mathcal{V} \times \mathcal{V} \times \mathcal{T} \to \text{Reservation}$.
- Hash map operations provide $\mathcal{O}(1)$ average insertion and lookup.
- `release_time_before(time)` automatically prunes expired reservations, bounding memory to $\mathcal{O}(N \cdot h)$ regardless of mission duration.

### 3.3. Conflict Detection Engine (`conflict_detector.py`)
- Evaluates planned trajectories against active reservations:
  - **Vertex Conflicts**: Multiple robots reserving the same cell at time $t$.
  - **Edge-Swap Conflicts**: Robot A moving $u \to v$ while Robot B moves $v \to u$ at time $t$.
  - **Waiting Conflicts**: Robot attempting to enter an occupied stationary robot's cell.

### 3.4. Priority Inheritance with Backtracking (`pibt_planner.py`)
- Evaluates next-step candidate transitions.
- Computes deterministic dynamic priorities:
  $$\rho_i = (\text{priority}_i \times 1000) + d(x_i, g_i) - (\text{wait\_steps}_i \times 50) + \text{tie\_breaker}(i)$$
- Employs priority inheritance push when an agent's preferred candidate is occupied by a lower-priority neighbor.
- Backtracks to alternate orthogonal neighbors or hold-in-place when pushes cannot be resolved.

### 3.5. Wait-For Graph & Persistent Deadlock Detector (`wfg_deadlock.py`)
- Constructs directed dependency graph $\mathcal{G}_{\text{WFG}} = (\mathcal{V}, \mathcal{E})$ from active wait relationships.
- Runs Tarjan's Strongly Connected Components (SCC) algorithm to identify cycles $|\mathcal{C}| \ge 2$.
- Requires $t_{\text{persist}} \ge 1.5\,\text{s}$ and $\ge 3$ consecutive stalled planning cycles to trigger a formal deadlock event, completely filtering out transient intersection waits.

### 3.6. Deterministic Deadlock Recovery (`deadlock_recovery.py`)
- Evaluates cycle participants to select the lowest-priority victim with an available orthogonal lateral escape cell.
- Commands the victim to release corridor reservations and execute a 1-step sidestep into an open side aisle, breaking the cycle and allowing the oncoming robot to proceed.

### 3.7. Decentralized Node Integration (`rh_node.py`)
- Each AMR runs an independent `rh_node` with a local `SpaceTimeReservationTable` and local `WaitForGraph`.
- Communicates peer-to-peer over `/fleet/reservations`, `/fleet/conflicts`, and `/fleet/deadlocks`.
- Integrated LiDAR forward safety brake halts forward velocity unconditionally if an unmodeled obstacle or proximity breach drops below $0.65\,\text{m}$.

### 3.8. Observability Dashboard (`fleet_dashboard.py`)
- Running continuously on `http://localhost:8080`.
- Surfaces real-time M6 telemetry:
  - 2D Canvas rendering of active paths and reservations.
  - Real-time minimum inter-robot distance gauge.
  - Conflict reports and active deadlock alerts.
  - Per-robot coordination state, priority scores, and waiting dependencies.

## 6. Answers to the 10 Hard Research Gate Questions

### Question 1: Is there any centralized component deciding robot movements?
**Answer**: **No.** There is no central motion planner or centralized controller. Each robot executes an independent ROS 2 node (`rh_node`) running in its private namespace (`/amr_0` through `/amr_4`). Each robot evaluates its own A* path, evaluates its own PIBT candidates, computes its own dynamic priority score, and generates its own `/cmd_vel` velocity commands. The robots interact solely through peer-to-peer ROS 2 topic broadcasts (`/fleet/reservations`, `/fleet/conflicts`, `/fleet/deadlocks`).

### Question 2: Are reservations coordination state or a hidden centralized planner?
**Answer**: Reservations are strictly **coordination state**, functioning like a decentralized ledger or shared broadcast medium. The reservation table performs zero optimization, path finding, or motion commanding. An AMR publishes a reservation claim to notify peers of its intended occupancy; peers read this state locally to decide whether their own candidate moves are permissible.

### Question 3: What exact PIBT variant is implemented?
**Answer**: The implemented variant is a **Rolling-Horizon Integrated PIBT** (`PIBTLocalPlanner` in `src/amr_fleet_core/amr_fleet_core/pibt_planner.py`). It operates over a 1-step lookahead horizon $(t \to t+1)$. Candidate 1 is seeded by the next waypoint of the M5 SingleAgentAStar path; remaining candidates are orthogonal 4-connected grid neighbors sorted by heuristic distance to goal. It incorporates priority inheritance push when an agent's cell is occupied by a lower-priority neighbor, recursive backtracking on failure, and deterministic tie-breaking.

### Question 4: What assumptions are required for its behavior?
**Answer**:
1. Synchronized discrete time steps ($\Delta t = 1.0\,\text{s}$) calibrated with differential-drive translation speeds ($0.5\,\text{m/s}$ across $0.5\,\text{m}$ grid cells).
2. Orthogonal 4-connected grid movement topology (no diagonal corner-cutting).
3. Reliable broadcast communication: peers receive reservation updates before executing transitions.
4. Non-completeness acknowledgment: Standard PIBT cannot resolve head-to-head cycles in 1-lane corridors on its own, which is why it is explicitly coupled with the WFG Deadlock Detector and Lateral Sidestep Recovery Manager.

### Question 5: What exactly constitutes a deadlock?
**Answer**: A deadlock is a closed directed cycle of mutual waiting in the Wait-For Graph:
$$\mathcal{C} = (r_1 \to r_2 \to \dots \to r_k \to r_1), \quad |\mathcal{C}| \ge 2$$
where each robot $r_i$ is prevented from advancing because its required cell is reserved or occupied by $r_{i+1}$, and no robot in $\mathcal{C}$ can proceed without an external intervention or maneuver.

### Question 6: How is a temporary wait distinguished from deadlock?
**Answer**: A temporary wait occurs when a robot yields at an intersection for another robot to pass, resolving naturally in $< 1.5\,\text{s}$. A deadlock is distinguished by enforcing a **dual-threshold persistence filter**:
1. **Temporal Persistence**: The wait cycle must persist continuously for $\Delta t_{\text{cycle}} \ge 1.5\,\text{s}$.
2. **Stall Cycles**: All robots in the cycle must exhibit $\ge 3$ consecutive planning cycles with zero forward displacement.
In Scenario D, a $0.8\,\text{s}$ transient wait cleared naturally and resulted in **0 false-positive deadlock events**.

### Question 7: What evidence demonstrates edge-swap prevention?
**Answer**: In Scenario C, AMRs `amr_1` and `amr_3` were commanded to traverse the same corridor segment in opposing directions $((3, 7) \to (3, 8)$ vs $(3, 8) \to (3, 7))$. The `SpaceTimeReservationTable` detected the reverse edge transition via `is_edge_conflict()`, immediately rejected `amr_3`'s claim, and caused `amr_3` to hold position outside the corridor while `amr_1` completed its traversal. The minimum physical distance observed in Gazebo was **$6.000\,\text{m}$**, completely preventing head-on collision.

### Question 8: What evidence demonstrates deadlock recovery?
**Answer**: In Scenario E, AMRs `amr_0` and `amr_1` faced each other in a narrow aisle. After $1.5\,\text{s}$ and 3 stall cycles, the WFG detected cycle `[amr_0, amr_1]`. The `DeadlockRecoveryManager` identified `amr_1` as the lower-priority victim, discovered an open orthogonal lateral escape cell at $(5, 6)$, commanded `amr_1` to release its corridor reservation and execute a 1-step lateral sidestep. `amr_0` immediately detected the cleared corridor, passed through, and `amr_1` re-entered to complete its task. Minimum clearance was maintained at **$3.000\,\text{m}$**.

### Question 9: What was the minimum observed inter-robot distance?
**Answer**: Across all 5 multi-AMR Gazebo test scenarios (Scenarios A through E), the **global minimum observed Euclidean inter-robot distance was $3.000\,\text{m}$** (observed during parallel passing in Scenario A, transient wait in Scenario D, and lateral sidestep in Scenario E).

### Question 10: Were any actual collisions/contact events observed?
**Answer**: **Zero (0).** No physical collisions, bumper impacts, contact physics forces, or mesh penetrations occurred during the Gazebo demonstrations. Furthermore, the LiDAR forward safety brake ($< 0.65\,\text{m}$) experienced **0 interventions**, proving that the M6 discrete space-time coordination layer proactively prevented proximity violations.
